Why fusing weak signals beats one strong model

notes · document forensics · ensembling

When I started building a fake PAN-card detector, the obvious approach was to train one strong end-to-end classifier: feed in a document image, get out a suspicious/clean label. It seemed simpler, and in a lot of ML problems it's the right instinct. For document fraud specifically, it turned out to be the wrong one, and the reason is worth spelling out, because it generalizes past this one project.

The problem with one strong classifier

Forged documents don't fail in one consistent way. Some have OCR-corrupted or structurally invalid fields, a PAN number with a wrong taxpayer-entity code, a nonsensical date. Others look structurally fine but have a locally edited text region, a pasted photo, or a subtle sharpness mismatch between the tampered area and its surroundings. A single classifier trained end-to-end has to implicitly learn all of these failure modes from the same weight space, and in practice it tends to specialize on whichever signal is most common in the training data, quietly under-weighting the rarer forgery types, which are exactly the ones a real fraud team most needs caught.

Splitting the problem into independent checks

The alternative is to build several narrow, purpose-built detectors that each look for one failure mode, and combine their outputs:

  • OCR and rule validation: deterministic checks on extracted text: field structure, date validity, known-invalid patterns.
  • Regional tamper detection: compares local sharpness and edge characteristics of a text region against its surrounding context.
  • Visual forensics: looks for image-level artifacts: digital overlays, unusual noise patterns, error-level analysis inconsistencies.
  • Patch-level deep learning: a classifier scoring sliding-window patches for localized visual tampering the other three might miss.

None of these four checks is individually as accurate as a well-tuned end-to-end model would be on its best-case failure mode. But each one fails on a different subset of forgeries, and importantly, each fails in a different, mostly uncorrelated way.

Why voting beats stacking here

Rather than feeding all four signals into a fifth model to learn how to weight them (a stacking approach), a simple 2-of-4 voting scheme (flag a document as suspicious if at least two of the four stages fire) turned out to be both more robust and far more interpretable. Interpretability matters a lot in a fraud-review context: a reviewer can be told exactly which two checks fired and why, rather than trusting an opaque combined score. Voting is also less prone to a single miscalibrated stage dominating the outcome. A stacked model can learn to over-trust whichever input happens to correlate best with the training labels, even if that input is the least reliable one in production.

When this pattern generalizes

This isn't specific to document forgery. Anywhere you have several genuinely independent sources of weak evidence, and the cost of a false negative is high, an ensemble of narrow detectors with a simple, auditable combination rule is often both more robust and more trustworthy than a single model asked to do everything at once, even if that single model would score slightly higher on a clean benchmark.
